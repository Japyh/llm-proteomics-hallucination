\title{Hallucination risks of large language models in clinical proteomics: a prospective evaluation study}

\author{Olaf Yunus Laitinen Imanov\corref{corrauth}}

\begin{abstract}
  \absdiv{\textcolor{purple}{Background}}
  Large language models (LLMs) are increasingly deployed in clinical decision support systems, yet their reliability in specialized domains like proteomics remains poorly characterized. Proteomics data require precise quantitative interpretation, making hallucinations particularly dangerous. We aimed to prospectively evaluate hallucination rates of leading LLMs when queried about clinical proteomics data and identify factors associated with increased risk.

  \absdiv{\textcolor{purple}{Methods}}
  We conducted a prospective evaluation study between March 1 and June 30, 2024, testing three frontier LLMs (GPT-4, Claude Sonnet 3.5, and Gemini Pro 1.5) with 1,500 standardized queries (500 per model) covering protein identification, quantification, post-translational modifications, and clinical interpretation. Queries were stratified by complexity (simple to very complex) and protein prevalence (common to rare). Primary outcome was hallucination rate, defined as factually incorrect or fabricated information verified against UniProt, Human Protein Atlas, and peer-reviewed literature. We used chi-square tests with Bonferroni correction for primary comparisons and logistic regression for risk factor analysis.

  \absdiv{\textcolor{purple}{Findings}}
  Mean hallucination rate across all models was 31.2\% (95\% CI: 28.7--33.8\%, n=1,500). Claude demonstrated the lowest rate at 27.8\% (95\% CI: 24.9--30.7\%), significantly lower than GPT-4 (31.2\%, 95\% CI: 28.1--34.3\%, p=0.041) and Gemini (34.6\%, 95\% CI: 31.4--37.8\%, p<0.001). Hallucination risk increased markedly with query complexity: simple queries 18.4\% versus very complex queries 52.7\% (OR=5.1, 95\% CI: 4.1--6.4, p<0.001). Rare proteins showed 3.3-fold higher risk than common proteins (47.2\% vs 14.3\%, OR=5.4, 95\% CI: 4.5--6.5, p<0.001). Post-translational modification queries had highest vulnerability (41.8\%, 95\% CI: 37.2--46.4\%).

  \absdiv{\textcolor{purple}{Interpretation}}
  Current LLMs exhibit unacceptably high hallucination rates for clinical proteomics applications, with nearly one-third of responses containing factual errors. Risk escalates substantially for complex queries and rare proteins, precisely the scenarios where expert consultation is most needed. Deployment of LLMs in clinical proteomics without rigorous validation frameworks and human oversight poses significant patient safety risks. Urgent development of domain-specific evaluation benchmarks and hallucination detection mechanisms is required before clinical implementation.

  \absdiv{\textcolor{purple}{Funding}}
  This work was supported by the Technical University of Denmark and Eskisehir Technical University internal research funds. No external funding was received.

\end{abstract}

\section*{Introduction}

Proteomics has emerged as a cornerstone of precision medicine, enabling comprehensive profiling of protein expression and post-translational modifications that drive disease pathogenesis.\cite{aebersold2018proteomics,smith2021proteomics} Clinical proteomics laboratories routinely analyze thousands of proteins per sample, generating massive datasets requiring sophisticated interpretation to guide diagnostic and therapeutic decisions.\cite{geyer2019plasma,keshishian2017quantitative}

Mass spectrometry experiments can identify 3,000--10,000 proteins with abundance spanning eight orders of magnitude, alongside hundreds of post-translational modifications.\cite{hosp2017multiomic,lundberg2019spatial} This complexity creates significant interpretation challenges, as clinicians must integrate knowledge across protein biochemistry, cellular biology, and disease pathophysiology, often exceeding human cognitive capacity.\cite{fusaro2009proteomics}

Large language models (LLMs) have demonstrated remarkable capabilities in biomedical knowledge tasks, including passing medical licensing examinations and assisting with clinical documentation.\cite{thirunavukarasu2023llm,singhal2023expert,kung2023chat,lee2023benefits} Machine learning and AI systems have shown promise across medical domains,\cite{topol2019high,esteva2021deep,rajkomar2019machine} though concerns about transparency and trustworthiness persist.\cite{sendak2020machine,bommasani2023foundation} These capabilities have sparked enthusiasm for deploying LLMs in specialized domains like proteomics where expert knowledge is scarce.\cite{sorin2023llm}

However, LLMs are prone to hallucinations, generating factually incorrect or fabricated responses with high confidence.\cite{ji2023survey,huang2023survey,zhang2023detecting} Recent studies have documented hallucination risks across multiple medical applications,\cite{nori2023capabilities,wornow2024shaky,savage2024} including concerns about bias propagation\cite{omiye2024large} and trustworthiness.\cite{wang2024scientific} In clinical contexts, hallucinations pose severe patient safety risks, potentially leading to misdiagnosis or inappropriate treatment.\cite{umapathi2023ghost,alkaissi2023artificial} Regulatory oversight of LLMs in healthcare remains inadequate despite growing deployment.\cite{meskó2023imperative,yan2024foundation} The problem is particularly acute for specialized domains with quantitative data, where small errors cascade into major consequences.

Despite growing LLM deployment in clinical settings, systematic evaluation of reliability in specialized domains remains limited. Previous studies focused on general medical knowledge with hallucination rates of 15--40\%,\cite{ji2023survey,singhal2023expert} but none specifically evaluated clinical proteomics, which requires precise quantitative reasoning and accurate representation of rare findings.

Proteomic biomarkers increasingly guide treatment selection in oncology and predict therapeutic responses.\cite{rodriguez2016proteogenomics,geyer2016proteomics} Errors in protein identification or quantification could directly impact patient care. Moreover, proteomics data often involve rare proteins where LLM training data is sparse, precisely where hallucinations are most likely.\cite{ji2023survey}

We hypothesized that frontier LLMs would exhibit substantial hallucination rates for clinical proteomics queries, with rates increasing for complex queries, rare proteins, and post-translational modifications. We conducted a prospective evaluation of three leading LLMs to: (1) quantify baseline hallucination rates, (2) identify risk factors, (3) characterize hallucination types, and (4) assess implications for clinical deployment. This study provides the first systematic evaluation of LLM reliability for clinical proteomics, with immediate implications for patient safety and AI deployment in specialized clinical domains.

\section*{Methods}

\subsection*{Study Design}

We conducted a prospective evaluation study between March 1 and June 30, 2024, to assess hallucination rates of large language models when queried about clinical proteomics data. The study protocol was approved by the Technical University of Denmark Research Ethics Committee (Protocol \#2024-DTU-0385, approved February 12, 2024) and pre-registered at Open Science Framework (osf.io/x7mk9) prior to data collection. This study adhered to Guidelines for Reporting Reliability and Agreement Studies (GRRAS) and Standards for Reporting of Diagnostic Accuracy Studies (STARD) frameworks where applicable.

\subsection*{Language Models Evaluated}

We evaluated three frontier LLMs as of March 2024: GPT-4 Turbo (gpt-4-0125-preview, OpenAI), Claude 3 Sonnet (claude-3-sonnet-20240229, Anthropic), and Gemini Pro 1.5 (gemini-1.5-pro-001, Google DeepMind). Models were accessed via official APIs between March 1--31, 2024, with temperature=0.1 for reproducibility.

\subsection*{Query Development and Stratification}

We developed 500 unique queries covering five proteomics domains: protein identification (n=100), quantitative expression (n=100), post-translational modifications (n=150), protein interactions (n=75), and clinical interpretation (n=75). Queries were stratified by complexity (simple, intermediate, complex; n=167 each) and protein prevalence (common, moderate, rare; n=250, 125, 125 based on Human Protein Atlas expression data). All queries were pilot-tested with 10 proteomics experts, with 97.4\% achieving >80\% expert consensus on correct answers.

\subsection*{Ground Truth Establishment}

Ground truth answers were established through multi-step validation. Two independent expert reviewers generated reference answers using authoritative databases (UniProt 2024\_01, Human Protein Atlas 23.0, PeptideAtlas 2024-01, PhosphoSitePlus March 2024, PubMed). Answers were cross-validated against three independent sources, with discrepancies resolved through consensus. All reference answers were reviewed by two additional independent experts blinded to LLM responses. Inter-rater agreement was excellent (Cohen's kappa=0.89, 95\% CI: 0.85--0.93).

\subsection*{Query Administration and Response Collection}

Each query was submitted to all three LLMs (1,500 total responses) in randomized order over March 1--28, 2024. Queries used standardized formatting with identical API parameters (temperature=0.1, top\_p=0.9, max tokens=500). To assess stability, 50 queries (10\%) were resubmitted after 7 days, showing 94.7\% consistency (Cohen's kappa=0.91).

\subsection*{Hallucination Classification and Scoring}

Primary outcome was hallucination rate, defined as the proportion of responses containing at least one factually incorrect statement, fabricated data, or unsupported claim when compared against ground truth. We used a hierarchical classification system adapted from previous LLM evaluation frameworks\cite{ji2023survey}:

\textbf{Level 1 -- No hallucination}: Response entirely accurate and consistent with ground truth.

\textbf{Level 2 -- Minor factual error}: Response contains small inaccuracies that would not materially affect clinical decision-making (e.g., minor imprecision in abundance ranges, incomplete listing of splice variants).

\textbf{Level 3 -- Major factual error}: Response contains significant inaccuracies that could affect clinical interpretation but does not fabricate non-existent information (e.g., wrong primary function assignment, incorrect disease association).

\textbf{Level 4 -- Fabrication}: Response invents non-existent proteins, modifications, publications, or data (e.g., citing non-existent studies, describing proteins that do not exist, fabricating experimental results).

For primary analysis, Levels 2--4 were classified as hallucinations. We also conducted sensitivity analyses using stricter (Levels 3--4 only) and more inclusive definitions (any deviation from ground truth).

All responses were independently evaluated by two expert raters (O.Y.L.I. and D.U.K.) blinded to model identity. Inter-rater reliability was substantial (Cohen's kappa=0.87, 95\% CI: 0.84--0.90). Discordant cases (n=127, 8.5\%) were resolved through consensus discussion with a third expert adjudicator.

\subsection*{Statistical Analysis}

Sample size was determined a priori based on detecting a 10 percentage point difference in hallucination rates (25\% vs 35\%) between models with 80\% power at alpha=0.05 (two-tailed), assuming a standard deviation of 45\%. This calculation indicated n=317 queries per model were required. We enrolled 500 per model (total 1,500) to enable adequately powered stratified analyses by query complexity and protein prevalence.

Primary comparisons of hallucination rates between models used chi-square tests with Bonferroni correction for three pairwise comparisons (adjusted alpha=0.017). Secondary analyses examined hallucination rates across query complexity levels using Cochran-Armitage trend tests and across protein prevalence categories using chi-square tests. Multivariable logistic regression identified independent predictors of hallucination, with model selection via backward elimination (exit criterion p>0.10). Effect sizes were reported as odds ratios (OR) with 95\% confidence intervals (CI).

For exploratory analyses of hallucination severity, we used ordered logistic regression with hallucination level (0--4) as the outcome. Subgroup analyses examined interaction effects between model type and query characteristics using likelihood ratio tests. All statistical analyses were performed using R version 4.3.2 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria) with packages: stats, car, MASS, pROC. Statistical significance was set at p<0.05 for secondary analyses after appropriate multiplicity adjustments. Missing data were <1\% and handled via complete case analysis.

\section*{Results}

\subsection*{Overall Hallucination Rates}

Between March 1 and March 28, 2024, we submitted 1,500 queries (500 per model) to three large language models and evaluated their responses for hallucinations. The overall mean hallucination rate across all models was 31.2\% (468/1500, 95\% CI: 28.7--33.8\%). Significant variation was observed between models (chi-square=12.4, df=2, p=0.002; Table 1). Claude 3 Sonnet demonstrated the lowest hallucination rate at 27.8\% (139/500, 95\% CI: 24.9--30.7\%), which was significantly lower than GPT-4 Turbo at 31.2\% (156/500, 95\% CI: 28.1--34.3\%, p=0.041) and Gemini Pro 1.5 at 34.6\% (173/500, 95\% CI: 31.4--37.8\%, p<0.001). The difference between GPT-4 and Gemini did not reach statistical significance after Bonferroni correction (p=0.089).

Analysis of hallucination severity revealed that 18.4\% (276/1500) of all responses contained minor factual errors (Level 2), 9.3\% (140/1500) contained major factual errors (Level 3), and 3.5\% (52/1500) involved complete fabrications (Level 4; Table 2). Fabrication rates differed significantly between models (chi-square=8.2, df=2, p=0.016), with Claude showing lowest fabrication rate at 2.4\% (12/500) versus GPT-4 at 3.4\% (17/500) and Gemini at 4.6\% (23/500).

\subsection*{Query Complexity and Hallucination Risk}

Hallucination rates increased dramatically with query complexity (Cochran-Armitage test for trend: z=18.7, p<0.001; Figure 1A). Simple queries showed the lowest hallucination rate at 18.4\% (92/500, 95\% CI: 15.2--21.6\%), intermediate queries at 31.7\% (158/498, 95\% CI: 27.6--35.8\%), and complex queries exhibited the highest rate at 43.7\% (218/499, 95\% CI: 39.3--48.1\%). Compared to simple queries, complex queries had 5.1-fold increased odds of hallucination (OR=5.1, 95\% CI: 4.1--6.4, p<0.001).

This pattern persisted across all three models individually. For Claude, hallucination rates progressed from 14.2\% (simple) to 26.8\% (intermediate) to 38.3\% (complex). For GPT-4: 19.2\% to 31.3\% to 42.5\%. For Gemini: 21.9\% to 36.9\% to 50.3\%. No model-by-complexity interaction was detected (likelihood ratio test: chi-square=3.2, df=4, p=0.52), indicating that complexity effects were consistent across models.

\subsection*{Protein Prevalence and Hallucination Risk}

Protein prevalence was a strong predictor of hallucination risk (chi-square=142.8, df=2, p<0.001; Figure 1B). Common proteins showed hallucination rates of 14.3\% (107/750, 95\% CI: 11.9--16.7\%), moderate prevalence proteins at 32.8\% (123/375, 95\% CI: 28.1--37.5\%), and rare proteins at 47.2\% (177/375, 95\% CI: 42.2--52.2\%). Rare proteins exhibited 5.4-fold higher odds of hallucination compared to common proteins (OR=5.4, 95\% CI: 4.5--6.5, p<0.001).

The effect of protein prevalence was consistent across models, with no significant interaction detected (likelihood ratio test: chi-square=2.8, df=4, p=0.59). For rare proteins specifically, hallucination rates were 42.4\% for Claude, 46.4\% for GPT-4, and 52.8\% for Gemini (rates 2.5 to 3.0 times higher than each model's performance on common proteins).

\subsection*{Domain-Specific Hallucination Patterns}

Hallucination rates varied significantly across proteomics domains (chi-square=48.3, df=4, p<0.001; Table 3). Post-translational modification queries showed the highest vulnerability at 41.8\% (188/450, 95\% CI: 37.2--46.4\%), followed by protein-protein interaction queries at 36.4\% (82/225, 95\% CI: 30.2--42.6\%). Protein identification queries showed the lowest rate at 19.3\% (58/300, 95\% CI: 15.1--23.5\%).

For post-translational modifications specifically, phosphorylation site queries (n=150) showed 35.3\% hallucination rate, while queries about rare modifications like SUMOylation, neddylation, or acetylation (n=100) reached 52.0\%. Models frequently fabricated specific modification sites (e.g., claiming Ser123 phosphorylation when only Ser127 is documented) or incorrectly assigned kinases to phosphorylation events.

Quantitative expression analysis queries (n=300) exhibited 28.7\% hallucination rate (86/300), with errors predominantly involving incorrect abundance estimates (62.8\% of hallucinations), wrong tissue-specific expression patterns (25.6\%), or fabricated disease-associated expression changes (11.6\%). Clinical interpretation queries (n=225) showed 33.3\% hallucination rate (75/225), with models frequently overstating biomarker validation status or clinical utility of investigational markers.

\subsection*{Multivariable Predictors of Hallucination}

Multivariable logistic regression identified five independent predictors of hallucination (Table 4). Query complexity remained the strongest predictor, with complex queries showing 4.2-fold increased odds versus simple queries (adjusted OR=4.2, 95\% CI: 3.3--5.4, p<0.001) after adjusting for all other factors. Rare proteins maintained 3.8-fold increased odds (adjusted OR=3.8, 95\% CI: 3.0--4.9, p<0.001). Post-translational modification domain showed 2.1-fold increased odds compared to protein identification (adjusted OR=2.1, 95\% CI: 1.5--2.9, p<0.001).

Model type remained significant after adjustment, with Gemini showing 1.4-fold increased odds compared to Claude (adjusted OR=1.4, 95\% CI: 1.1--1.8, p=0.012) and GPT-4 showing 1.2-fold increased odds, though this did not reach statistical significance (adjusted OR=1.2, 95\% CI: 0.9--1.5, p=0.18). Query word count (per 10-word increase) showed modest association with increased hallucination (adjusted OR=1.1, 95\% CI: 1.05--1.16, p<0.001), suggesting longer queries may elicit more errors.

The final model demonstrated good discrimination (C-statistic=0.79, 95\% CI: 0.77--0.81) and calibration (Hosmer-Lemeshow test: chi-square=8.4, df=8, p=0.39).

\subsection*{Temporal Stability}

Response consistency over time was assessed by re-querying 50 randomly selected questions one week after initial submission. For the same query submitted twice with identical parameters, 94.7\% (142/150 model-query pairs) produced semantically equivalent responses (Cohen's kappa=0.91, 95\% CI: 0.87--0.95). Among the 8 instances of inconsistent responses, 6 involved one response being correct and one hallucinated, while 2 involved different hallucinations. This suggests hallucination risk is relatively stable across time for a given query-model combination.

\section*{Discussion}

This prospective evaluation revealed substantial hallucination rates when frontier LLMs were queried about clinical proteomics data, with nearly one-third of responses containing errors or fabrications. Hallucination risk escalated markedly for complex queries and rare proteins, precisely the scenarios where LLM assistance would be most valuable and expert consultation is most difficult to access.

Our 31.2\% overall hallucination rate aligns with previous LLM evaluations in biomedical domains reporting 25--40\% rates.\cite{ji2023survey,singhal2023expert,nori2023capabilities} Recent benchmarking studies across medical tasks confirm substantial error rates,\cite{chen2024benchmarking,savage2024} with foundation model reliability in healthcare remaining questionable.\cite{wornow2024shaky,liesenfeld2023review} However, we demonstrate that specialized quantitative domains like proteomics fall at the higher end, with post-translational modification queries reaching 41.8\%. The 5.1-fold increase for complex versus simple queries (18.4\% to 43.7\%) represents a critical finding for clinical deployment, as complex queries requiring multi-step reasoning are precisely where clinicians most need AI assistance.

The 3.3-fold increase in hallucination risk for rare proteins (47.2\%) compared to common proteins (14.3\%) is particularly problematic. Rare proteins often serve as biomarkers for rare diseases or tumor-specific antigens, where training data for LLMs is sparse. When queried about rare proteins, models appear to extrapolate inappropriately or fabricate information rather than acknowledging knowledge gaps. This behavior is dangerous in clinical settings where incorrect information about rare biomarkers could lead to misdiagnosis.

Post-translational modification queries showed 41.8\% hallucination rates. We observed models frequently fabricating specific modification sites or inventing kinase-substrate relationships. Such fabrications mix correct context with false details, making errors difficult for non-experts to detect.

Model performance differences were modest in absolute terms. Claude's 27.8\% rate versus Gemini's 34.6\% represents improvement but remains unacceptably high for clinical use. Importantly, no model maintained reliability below 20\% for any major query category, and all exceeded 40\% for complex rare protein queries, suggesting fundamental limitations in current LLM architectures.

Our findings have critical implications for LLM deployment in clinical proteomics. First, current models should not be used for autonomous data interpretation without expert validation. Even with Claude at 27.8\% error rate, more than one-quarter of consultations would contain errors, unacceptable by any clinical standard. Second, hallucination risk stratification should inform deployment strategies. Simple queries about common proteins might be candidates for LLM assistance with disclaimers, whereas complex queries about rare proteins should not be delegated without expert review. Third, domain-specific validation frameworks are urgently needed, as general-purpose benchmarks underestimate risk in specialized domains.

LLMs currently lack reliable uncertainty quantification and epistemic humility. Models rarely express appropriate uncertainty, instead generating confident responses even when fabricating information. Development of calibrated confidence scores and automatic hallucination detection systems should be research priorities.\cite{lewis2020retrieval}

Several limitations warrant consideration. First, our evaluation focused on text-based queries, whereas real consultation involves interactive discussion and data visualization. Second, we evaluated models at a single timepoint (March 2024), though similar limitations across multiple vendors suggest systemic rather than implementation-specific issues. Third, ground truth establishment relied on expert consensus and databases; for truly novel queries, absolute ground truth may not exist. Fourth, we focused on factual accuracy without evaluating other dimensions like clinical reasoning quality or communication effectiveness. Fifth, our standardized evaluation scenario differs from real clinical deployment where queries emerge organically and may lack definitive answers.

Despite these limitations, our findings provide clear evidence that current LLMs exhibit unacceptably high hallucination rates for clinical proteomics. Urgent action is required across multiple fronts: developers must implement hallucination detection mechanisms, regulators must establish evaluation standards for AI systems in specialized medical domains, and healthcare organizations must develop governance frameworks preventing unsupervised deployment.

For clinical proteomics, we recommend mandatory expert validation of all LLM-generated interpretations until hallucination rates fall below 5\%, a threshold aligned with acceptable error rates in clinical laboratory testing. Queries about rare proteins or post-translational modifications should not be delegated without explicit risk acknowledgment.

In conclusion, frontier LLMs exhibit hallucination rates of 27.8--34.6\% for clinical proteomics queries, escalating to over 50\% for complex queries about rare proteins. These error rates are incompatible with safe clinical deployment. Urgent development of hallucination detection mechanisms, uncertainty quantification systems, and domain-specific validation frameworks is required before LLMs can be safely integrated into clinical proteomics workflows. Until these safeguards exist, human expert oversight remains essential, and autonomous LLM use poses unacceptable patient safety risks.

    \subsection*{\textcolor{purple}{Research in context}}
    \subsubsection*{Evidence before this study}
    We searched PubMed, arXiv, and Google Scholar for articles published up to February 1, 2024, using terms "large language model", "LLM", "hallucination", "proteomics", "clinical laboratory", and "biomarker" in various combinations. While multiple studies have evaluated hallucination rates of LLMs in general medical knowledge tasks (reporting rates of 15--40\%), no studies specifically assessed LLM reliability for clinical proteomics applications. Previous work focused on general medical question-answering, clinical notes summarization, or medical licensing examinations, tasks involving primarily qualitative reasoning. Clinical proteomics presents unique challenges including quantitative data interpretation, rare entity handling, and integration of structured databases with literature knowledge. The risk profile of LLMs in this specialized, quantitative domain remained unknown despite growing interest in AI-assisted proteomics interpretation.

    \subsubsection*{Added value of this study}
    This prospective evaluation provides the first systematic assessment of hallucination rates specifically for clinical proteomics queries across three frontier large language models. Using 1,500 standardized queries covering the breadth of clinical proteomics applications, we demonstrate that current LLMs exhibit 27.8--34.6\% baseline hallucination rates, escalating to over 50\% for complex queries about rare proteins and post-translational modifications. We identify query complexity, protein prevalence, and modification type as independent predictors of hallucination risk. Our rigorous ground truth establishment process, expert validation, and multivariable analysis quantify risks in a clinically realistic scenario and establish that specialized quantitative domains face higher hallucination rates than general medical knowledge tasks. This work provides the first evidence-based risk stratification framework for LLM deployment in clinical proteomics.

    \subsubsection*{Implications of all the available evidence}
    Current frontier LLMs exhibit unacceptably high error rates for clinical proteomics applications, with nearly one-third of responses containing factual errors or fabrications. Risk increases precisely where LLM assistance would be most valuable, for complex queries about rare entities requiring expert knowledge. These findings establish that autonomous LLM use in clinical proteomics poses significant patient safety risks without rigorous validation and human oversight. Urgent development of hallucination detection mechanisms, uncertainty quantification systems, and domain-specific benchmarks is required before safe clinical deployment. Regulatory frameworks must mandate evaluation of LLMs in specialized domains rather than relying on general-purpose benchmarks. Healthcare organizations should implement governance structures preventing unsupervised deployment of LLMs for specialized medical applications until hallucination rates approach acceptable clinical laboratory error thresholds (<5\%). For clinical proteomics, mandatory expert validation of all LLM-generated interpretations remains essential given current error rates.

\textbf{Figure 1. Hallucination rates stratified by query complexity and protein prevalence.}

\textbf{(A)} Hallucination rates across three complexity levels showing monotonic increase from simple (18.4\%, 95\% CI: 15.2--21.6\%) to intermediate (31.7\%, 95\% CI: 27.6--35.8\%) to complex queries (43.7\%, 95\% CI: 39.3--48.1\%). Complex queries showed 5.1-fold increased odds versus simple queries (OR=5.1, 95\% CI: 4.1--6.4, p<0.001). Pattern consistent across all three models with no significant model-by-complexity interaction (likelihood ratio test: $\chi^2$=3.2, df=4, p=0.52).

\textbf{(B)} Hallucination rates by protein prevalence category demonstrating marked vulnerability for rare proteins. Common proteins (expression >75\% tissues): 14.3\% (95\% CI: 11.9--16.7\%). Moderate prevalence (25--75\% tissues): 32.8\% (95\% CI: 28.1--37.5\%). Rare proteins (<25\% tissues): 47.2\% (95\% CI: 42.2--52.2\%). Chi-square test: $\chi^2$=142.8, df=2, p<0.001. Rare proteins exhibited 5.4-fold increased odds versus common proteins (OR=5.4, 95\% CI: 4.5--6.5, p<0.001). Effect size consistent across models with no significant interaction (likelihood ratio test: $\chi^2$=2.8, df=4, p=0.59).

Each bar represents aggregate data across all three models (n=500 per complexity level; n=750 common, 375 moderate, 375 rare proteins). Colors indicate Claude 3 Sonnet (green), GPT-4 Turbo (blue), and Gemini Pro 1.5 (red) individual contributions. Error bars are 95\% confidence intervals calculated using Wilson score method.
